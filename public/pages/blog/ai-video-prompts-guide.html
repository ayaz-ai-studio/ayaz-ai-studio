How to Write AI Video Prompts: Camera, Light and One Clear Action

November 12, 2026 · 10 min read

By Khan Ayaz — Ayaz AI Studio · 12 Nov 2026 · 10 min read

An image prompt describes a single frozen moment. A video prompt has a much harder job: it must direct a tiny film scene that lasts only a few seconds, in which something moves, the camera behaves in a particular way, and the light does something deliberate. When a prompt is vague, the model still has to make all of those decisions — it just makes them randomly, and you pay for the randomness in wasted generations. The good news is that video prompts have a structure. Learn the six parts, the camera vocabulary and the one-action rule, and your clips stop being lottery tickets and start being shots you directed.

The six parts of a working video prompt

Almost every reliable video prompt contains the same six ingredients, in roughly this order. Subject: who or what is on screen, described specifically — not "a dog" but "a golden retriever puppy". Action: the one thing the subject does, expressed with a clear verb. Setting: the place and time, in a few concrete words. Camera: how the camera moves and how the shot is framed. Light and mood: the quality of the light and the feeling it creates. Technical tail: duration, aspect ratio and style words, where your tool offers them. Assembled, the skeleton looks like this: [subject] [action] in [setting], [camera movement and framing], [lighting], [mood], [duration]. You will not always need all six, but when a clip disappoints, checking which ingredient was missing usually explains why.

Camera movement: the vocabulary that changes everything

Camera words are the highest-leverage words in a video prompt, because movement is what makes a clip feel filmed rather than generated. The core vocabulary is small. A static shot (or "locked-off camera") holds still and lets the subject move — best for dialogue and product shots. A slow push-in drifts toward the subject and builds intimacy or tension. A pull back reveals the surroundings. A pan sweeps left or right across a scene. A tracking shot moves alongside the subject, the classic walking or running shot. An orbit circles the subject, good for products and portraits. A handheld shot adds small natural shakes that read as realism, and an aerial rise lifts away like a drone. Pair movement with framing: close-up, medium shot, wide shot, low angle, over-the-shoulder. One firm rule: one camera move per clip. "Zoom in while orbiting while rising" asks the model to do three jobs at once, and the result is usually a wobble that does none of them.

Lighting and mood do the emotional heavy lifting

The same street can look hopeful, romantic or threatening depending entirely on the light, and video models respond strongly to lighting words. Give every prompt a light source and a time of day: "golden hour sunlight", "soft overcast daylight", "warm window light", "blue-grey rainy afternoon", "neon signs at night", "candlelight". Then add one mood word — calm, joyful, tense, lonely, majestic, eerie — as a steering hint for everything you did not specify: colour, pace, expression. Skipping the lighting is the most common reason two clips from similar prompts feel like they belong to different films. If a generation comes back looking flat, fixing the light usually fixes the clip faster than rewriting the subject.

The one-action rule, and why duration decides it

Most AI clips run somewhere between three and ten seconds. In that time, a subject can believably do one thing: walk to the door, or open it, or react to what is behind it — not all three. Prompts that narrate a whole little story ("she runs downstairs, grabs her bag, cycles to the station and boards the train") force the model to compress four shots into one, and it responds with morphing, skipped steps and melted details. Work like an editor instead: plan a sequence as a chain of single-action clips and cut them together. This is also why the duration belongs in the prompt or settings — a "5 seconds" clip should contain about five seconds of action, which is less than most beginners instinctively write. When in doubt, halve the action.

Three example prompts, broken down

Example 1 — playful animal clip. "A golden retriever puppy bounds across a green lawn toward the camera, ears flopping, bright morning sunlight, low tracking shot at puppy height, joyful playful mood, 5 seconds." The breakdown: the subject is specific (breed and age, not "a dog"); the action is single and directional (bounds toward the camera); the camera is low and tracking, which is what makes it feel like a pet video someone actually filmed; morning light plus "joyful" sets the grade and energy; the duration matches one burst of running.

Example 2 — moody street scene. "A man in a dark coat waits under a shop awning while rain pours on a neon-lit street at night, slow push-in from a wide shot to a medium shot, reflections on the wet pavement, quiet melancholic mood, cinematic, 6 seconds." Here the subject barely moves — waiting is the action — so the camera does the travelling, and the slow push-in is the entire drama. The reflections give the model something beautiful and concrete to render, and "melancholic" plus "cinematic" hold the colour and pace together.

Example 3 — product clip. "A glass perfume bottle on a marble surface rotates slowly as soft window light sweeps across it, static close-up camera, clean bright background, elegant calm mood, 4 seconds." Notice what is absent: no people, no story, no second action. Rotation plus moving light is enough motion for a product shot, and the locked camera keeps the bottle's shape — the thing that must not distort — perfectly stable.

Common mistakes that waste generations

The expensive errors repeat across almost every beginner. Writing a plot instead of a shot — covered above; think in shots. Contradictions: "a sunny midnight", "an empty crowded market", "running while standing still". The model averages contradictions into mush. Asking for readable text: shop signs, book covers and captions still come out garbled in most models; film the scene clean and add text later in an editor. Expecting the same face across clips from text alone: without a reference image, every generation casts a new actor. Negative-word stuffing: long lists of "no blur, no distortion, no extra fingers" work poorly in video prompts; describing clearly what you do want — "smooth slow movement, steady camera" — steers far better. Changing everything at once: when a clip fails, alter one element and retry, or you learn nothing about which word caused the failure.

Start from a still image when consistency matters

For characters, products, or any series of clips, the professional shortcut is image-to-video: first create or choose the still image — the exact face, costume, product and framing — and then animate it with a prompt that describes only the motion and camera. The model no longer has to invent the subject and the movement simultaneously, so identity and details hold together across a whole sequence. Frequently asked questions

How long should a video prompt be? Usually one to three sentences. Long enough to name the six parts, short enough that no two instructions fight. If your prompt needs a comma splice and a plot twist, it is describing several clips, not one.

Why did the camera ignore my movement word? Most often because the prompt asked for two movements, or buried the camera instruction after a long subject description. Keep one move, and place it early enough to be noticed.

Why do faces melt in my clips? Faces suffer when they are small in the frame and moving fast. Move the camera closer, slow the action down, or start from a still image so the face begins correct and only has to survive gentle motion.

Can I reuse a good prompt? Yes — treat your best prompts as templates. Save them, swap one variable at a time (subject, setting, light), and you will build a personal library that produces reliable results instead of fresh gambles.
