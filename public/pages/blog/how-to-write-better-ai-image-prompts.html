How to Write Better AI Image Prompts: A Practical Guide

By Khan Ayaz — Ayaz AI Studio · October 5, 2026 · 9 min read

Ask two people to generate "a cat" with an AI image tool and you will get two completely different results. Ask someone who understands prompting, and you will get something that looks like it belongs in a magazine. The model is the same — the difference is the prompt. Good prompting is not magic and it is not luck; it is a skill with a structure you can learn in one sitting.

1. Start with a concrete subject

Every strong prompt begins with a specific subject, not a vague idea. "A dog" gives the model almost nothing to work with. "An elderly golden retriever with a grey muzzle, sitting on a wooden porch" gives it a character, an age, a setting. Specificity is the single highest-leverage thing you can add. Name the subject, its key features, and what it is doing. If there are multiple subjects, describe their relationship: who is in front, who is behind, who is looking at whom.

2. Name the style explicitly

AI models default to a generic digital-art look unless you tell them otherwise. If you want photorealism, say "photorealistic". If you want a watercolor, an oil painting, a 3D render, a film still, or a pencil sketch — say so, in the first sentence if possible. Style words act like a lens: they change everything downstream, from texture to color palette. You can also reference eras and movements ("1970s film photography", "ukiyo-e woodblock print") but keep it to one style per prompt; mixing three styles usually produces a muddy compromise.

3. Direct the light

Lighting is what separates amateur-looking generations from professional ones. "Soft morning light through a window", "dramatic side lighting", "neon glow at night", "golden hour backlight" — each of these completely changes the mood. Photographers obsess over light for a reason, and the same vocabulary works on AI models. Add the time of day, the light source, and the mood you want: warm and cozy, cold and clinical, dramatic and high-contrast.

4. Use camera and composition language

Terms borrowed from photography are extremely effective: "close-up portrait", "wide-angle landscape", "aerial view", "shallow depth of field", "rule of thirds", "low-angle shot". These tell the model how to frame the scene. "Shallow depth of field" reliably produces that blurred-background portrait look. "Wide-angle" pulls the scene open. If you want a poster-style image, add "centered composition, symmetrical".

5. Add texture and detail words

Once the big decisions are made, sprinkle in material and detail words: "weathered wood", "morning dew on grass", "intricate embroidery", "film grain", "highly detailed". These push the model toward richness instead of flat, plasticky surfaces. A little goes a long way — five or six detail words are plenty.

6. Know what to leave out

Longer is not always better. Prompts stuffed with twenty adjectives often confuse the model, which then drops half of them or blends them strangely. Aim for 30–60 words: one clear subject, one style, lighting, framing, and a handful of details. If the result ignores part of your prompt, that part was probably competing with something else — simplify and try again.

The second prompt makes dozens of decisions the first one leaves to chance. That is the whole game.

A practice routine that works

Pick one subject and generate it five times, changing only one element each time — first the style, then the lighting, then the framing. Save the versions you like and note which words caused the change. Within a week you will have an intuition for which words move the needle. Keep a small personal "prompt notebook" of phrases that worked; most experienced creators reuse their best fragments constantly.

Prompting well is the closest thing to a superpower in the AI art world. The models keep getting better, but the person who can describe exactly what they want will always get better results than the person who cannot. Start specific, direct the light, speak camera — and iterate.

Working across different tools

Prompting dialects differ slightly between model families. Some respond strongly to photographic vocabulary; others prefer plain descriptive language. If you switch tools, expect to recalibrate: a prompt that sings on one model may underperform on another. The fix is quick — regenerate your three best prompts on the new tool and note what changes. Within a day you will have translated your style. The underlying principles (specific subject, named style, directed light, camera language) transfer everywhere; only the seasoning changes.

Build a personal prompt recipe book

Professionals rarely write prompts from scratch. They maintain a small library of proven fragments: a lighting recipe that always looks expensive, a portrait formula, a landscape opener. When starting a new piece, they assemble fragments and adjust. Start yours today: every time a generation delights you, save the exact prompt in a note with the image. After a month you will own a toolkit that compounds — each new project starts from your best past work instead of a blank box.
