Photorealistic AI Portraits: A Complete Guide

By Khan Ayaz — Ayaz AI Studio · October 8, 2026 · 9 min read

Nothing exposes an AI image faster than a bad portrait. Human brains are ruthless face-detectors — we notice wrong eyes, plastic skin, and impossible lighting instantly. The good news: photorealistic portraits are one of the most solved problems in AI imagery, provided you prompt for reality instead of perfection.

1. Imperfect skin beats perfect skin

The number-one giveaway of AI portraits is airbrushed, poreless skin. Real skin has pores, fine lines, subtle asymmetry, and uneven tone. Prompt for it explicitly: "natural skin texture, visible pores, subtle imperfections". Add "film grain" and "candid" to break the studio-perfect sheen. Paradoxically, asking for flaws is what makes faces believable.

2. Light like a photographer

Flat, even lighting looks artificial. Real portraits are shaped by directional light: "soft window light from the left", "golden hour rim light on hair", "overcast daylight". Name one main light source and let shadows exist — deep but soft shadows around the nose and jaw are what make a face feel three-dimensional. Avoid "studio lighting" unless you truly want a corporate headshot look.

3. Speak lens language

"85mm portrait lens, shallow depth of field, f/1.8" is the magic trio for flattering portraits. The 85mm focal length avoids the distortion of wide angles; shallow depth of field melts the background into creamy bokeh and hides background defects the model might otherwise invent. For environmental portraits, try "35mm, environmental portrait" to keep context sharp.

4. Eyes are everything

Viewers look at eyes first, so protect them: add "sharp focus on eyes, detailed irises, natural catchlights" to the prompt and "asymmetric eyes, deformed eyes, cross-eyed" to the negative prompt. Catchlights — the tiny reflections of the light source — are a small detail with a huge realism payoff. If the eyes are wrong, no amount of good skin will save the portrait.

5. Age, ethnicity, and character

Generic prompts produce generic faces. Give your subject a specific age and lived-in details: "woman in her sixties, silver-streaked hair, laugh lines". Describe ethnicity and features respectfully and specifically rather than relying on stereotypes. Character — a scar, freckles, weathered hands — is what turns a face into a person.

6. Common mistakes that scream "AI"

  • Teeth: too many, too perfect, or melting into lips. Negate "bad teeth" and keep smiles subtle.
  • Ears and hands: check them; they are the model's weak points. Crop hands out when possible.
  • Hair: helmet-like solid hair looks fake — ask for "individual strands, flyaway hairs".
  • Backgrounds: warped text on signs and melting crowds. Use blurred backgrounds to hide them.
  • Over-smoothing: the "beauty filter" look. Counter with "unretouched, raw photo".

7. A reusable portrait formula

Candid photorealistic portrait of [subject with age and features], [expression], [lighting], 85mm lens, shallow depth of field, natural skin texture with visible pores, film grain, sharp focus on eyes

Fill in the brackets, add a negative prompt for anatomy defects, and you have a starting point that works surprisingly often on the first try.

Ethics note

Photorealistic faces carry responsibility. Never generate realistic portraits of real private individuals without consent, and never create deceptive imagery — fake endorsements, fabricated events, or impersonation. Most platforms, including ours, prohibit this. Create freely, but create honestly.

Group shots and full-body portraits

Every additional person multiplies the difficulty — faces shrink, hands multiply, interactions get strange. For group shots, keep poses simple and separated: "three friends standing side by side, arms at sides" beats complex intertwined poses. For full-body portraits, the face becomes small and the model has less detail budget for it; compensate with "detailed face" in the prompt and inspect the face closely before accepting the image. When in doubt, generate the portrait first and extend the scene around it rather than generating tiny faces.

The post-processing touch

Even great generations benefit from thirty seconds of finishing: slight contrast, a touch of grain, and a subtle vignette sell "photograph" to the eye. Free editors do all three. One caution — heavy filters can push an image back into uncanny territory; aim for the look of an unedited phone photo, not a magazine cover. The goal is believability, and believability whispers.

Working with reference faces

Some tools let you supply a reference face for consistency across images — invaluable for character work and storytelling. Use your own face or AI-generated faces, never real people's photos without permission. Even with consent, keep generations clearly labeled in commercial contexts; audiences deserve to know when a face is synthetic.

Backgrounds that do not betray you

The fastest way to ruin a great portrait is a background the model hallucinated badly: warped bookshelves, melting crowds, impossible architecture. Two fixes work reliably. First, blur it: "shallow depth of field, creamy bokeh background" hides a multitude of sins. Second, simplify it: "plain studio backdrop" or "soft gradient background" removes the problem entirely. Detailed environments are for environmental portraits where the setting is the point; everywhere else, the face deserves all the attention.
